What is SADL?

Supply And Demand Loss (SADL) addresses representation collapse in joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs). Regularizing the projector output does not guarantee that the encoder preserves differences between inputs. SADL measures those differences and their transmission to the output, then corrects the encoder when supply falls below demand.

The original task loss, output regularizer, and model architecture remain in place. We evaluate SADL in world-model control with LeWM and INTACT, and in representation learning on four image datasets.

MOTIVATION

Output constraints can miss
collapse inside the encoder.

In a JEPA, an encoder maps observations to H, and a projector maps H to Z. Prediction and control depend on those representations distinguishing different states.

In the models we study, a component shared by different inputs dominates H from initialization. During training, batch normalization in the projector removes a shift shared by all samples. Losses on Z therefore cannot suppress this shared component in H.

The remaining sample differences grow mainly through task gradients and stay concentrated in a few directions. In the LeWM baseline at epoch 1, they carry only 3.3% of the energy in H, with an effective rank of 14.5.

The projector can amplify existing differences.
It cannot recover information the encoder has lost.

METHOD

Compare demand with supply.
Correct the encoder when needed.

Demand is read from the projector output. Supply measures how much sample variation the encoder retains and how well it reaches the output.

01

Read the demand

Read how well Z satisfies its regularizer and how much sample variation it contains. Combine these into demand q, computed from the current representations.

q = √(qreg qexc)

02

Measure the weaker supply

Score the energy and spread of sample differences in H as kexc, and their transmission to Z as ktr. The weaker term determines supply k.

k = min{kexc, ktr}

03

Correct the encoder

When supply is below demand, apply the one-sided penalty directly to H. Once supply is sufficient, the extra penalty switches off.

S = [log(q + ε) − log(k + ε)]+2

The task loss and chosen output regularizer train as before. In the added term, sg stops gradients through Z, so the supply correction acts directly on H. The two weights set the strengths of output regularization and the supply correction.

Why correct only when supply is insufficient?

Direct regularization on H also works when its weight is right. The difference is how much the result depends on that weight. The always-on coding-rate baseline uses exactly SADL's excitation function, with a fixed weight and without demand or the one-sided gate.

OUTPUT GEOMETRY

One correction works with
different output regularizers.

SADL keeps the chosen output regularizer rather than prescribing one target shape. With eight regularizers, INTACT reaches 92.33–95.81% mean search-free success after one epoch; seven exceed 95%.

The output geometry can change while the supply correction stays the same. The glyph experiments illustrate this flexibility beyond the eight regularizers.
